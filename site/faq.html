What does the clock count?

Money paid to support renewable electricity in the UK since 2002. Directly: payments under Contracts for Difference, the Renewables Obligation and Feed-in Tariffs, and payments to wind farms to switch off. Indirectly: the extra cost of the grid and of balancing it, the carbon cost in the price of power, the Capacity Market levy, the Climate Change Levy on electricity, and the VAT households pay on all of these. The indirect costs are modelled estimates, and marked as such. The switch under the clock shows the direct payments alone.

Where do the figures come from?

The direct payments come from the bodies that record them: LCCC for Contracts for Difference and the Capacity Market, Ofgem for the Renewables Obligation and Feed-in Tariffs, Elexon for payments to wind farms to switch off and to other stations to switch on. Grid and balancing charges come from Ofgem, National Grid, the NAO and NESO; the Climate Change Levy from HMRC; inflation from ONS. The carbon line, households’ share of each cost for VAT, and every comparison with a Britain without net-zero policy come from the model behind Reliable Britain. Constraint payments for 2010 to 2023 are the Renewable Energy Foundation’s totals, £1.9bn of the figure. The methodology gives every source.

What does “indirect” mean, and how is it worked out?

A cost that renewables cause and billpayers pay, but that is not paid to renewable generators. Each is measured against what the same year would have cost in a Britain without net-zero policy: the grid it would have needed, the balancing it would have needed, the backup it would have bought. Only the difference is counted. The comparison comes from the model behind Reliable Britain.

Why does it keep ticking?

Every source publishes in arrears. The clock shows each source’s published figure up to its latest data, then carries that line forward at its latest yearly rate until the next figure arrives, when the published number replaces the estimate. The estimate is a small part of the total today.

Why 2024 prices?

So that a pound paid in 2002 and a pound paid in 2026 count the same. Each year is converted with ONS’s CPIH index. Untick “Adjust for inflation” to see the money as it was actually paid.

How does this relate to Reliable Britain?

They use the same sources and the same model, so their shared figures agree. Reliable Britain asks what electricity would have cost on gas; the clock counts what was paid. The clock covers the UK rather than Great Britain, counts the whole Climate Change Levy on electricity, includes biomass Contracts for Difference, and works out VAT a little differently.

Isn’t fossil fuel subsidised more?

The Fossil Fuel Clock, at fossilcost.com, counts £17.5bn a year. Its source is Global Justice Now (May 2025), which uses the OECD’s definition: tax charged below the standard rate counts as support. Its own table shows what the figure is made of. £6.9bn is VAT on household energy at 5% rather than 20% (£6.0bn on gas and other fuels, £0.9bn on electricity). £4.7bn is fuel duty relief, £1.6bn is relief from the Climate Change Levy, and £2.7bn is tax relief for oil and gas producers. That is £15.9bn, nine-tenths of the total, in tax not collected. A further £0.9bn is support for carbon capture.

The Subsidy Clock counts what billpayers and taxpayers actually paid. The Fossil Fuel Clock counts, for the most part, tax that was not charged. On its definition every reduced rate is support for what it covers: food, children’s clothes, and the 5% VAT on household electricity, whether it comes from gas or from wind and sun.

The Fossil Fuel Clock takes that one current estimate, built on 2023 data, and multiplies it back to 2002, though its source shows the total changing from year to year. Beside it, a second counter totals deaths from air pollution and leaves the other side of the ledger empty: the longer lives, and the steep fall in deaths from cold, heat, storm and flood, that cheap, reliable energy has made possible.

Its own code describes the £17.5bn as including “the cost of pollution / environmental damage”; its source’s table has no such line. It names no author, the three links in its sources list lead nowhere, and it welcomes corrections without saying where to send them.
