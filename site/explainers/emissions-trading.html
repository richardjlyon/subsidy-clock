What is it?

Generators that burn gas or coal pay for the carbon they emit. They buy an allowance for every tonne of CO₂, and since April 2013 they have also paid Carbon Price Support, a tax on their fuel. They build that cost into the price of their power. The most expensive plant running sets the price for everyone, so the carbon cost raises the price of all electricity sold, not just power from fossil plant.

Why does it exist?

Carbon pricing is the textbook tool for cutting emissions: make emitting expensive and cleaner options win on price. Only part of what it adds to the price reaches the Treasury.

What has it cost?

in cash as paid since 2005, currently growing at about a year — every second. Spread across Britain's households, that is about per household per year, reaching you through the wholesale price of power. Of the total, is tax collected by the Treasury: Carbon Price Support and the power sector's share of emissions-trading auctions. The other is the rest of the carbon in the price, which generators kept. From 2005 to 2012 generators were given most of their allowances free, but the carbon price still set the price of power, so the cost still reached bills. Those years count, as in Reliable Britain.

How sure are we?

The tax part comes from published receipts: HMRC's Carbon Price Support receipts and the emissions-trading auction results. For 2013 to 2020 the power sector's share of the auctions is estimated. The rest is modelled: the engine behind Reliable Britain works out, year by year, how much carbon pricing raised the wholesale price of power in Great Britain. Attribution confidence is medium.
