Methodology & sources

Every figure on the counter traces to an official source. This page sets out, scheme by scheme, where the data comes from, how the numbers are computed, how often they update, and what the known limitations are.

Contracts for Difference (CfD)

Source: LCCC, Actual CfD Generation and Avoided GHG Emissions · cadence: daily · CfD – renewables, including biomass; nuclear is tracked separately and appears once Hinkley Point C generates

Formula in words: the Low Carbon Contracts Company publishes the daily payment made under each individual CfD contract. We sum these per-contract daily payments. When the market reference price exceeds a contract’s strike price the daily payment is negative — the generator pays back — and these negative payments are netted against positive ones. Both figures are computed: the gross total (positive payments only) and the net total (after paybacks). The counter and all headline figures use the net total, which is the lower of the two.

The recipient table covers the CfD schemes (from LCCC per-generator payments) and the Renewables Obligation (named generating stations from David Turver’s ROC reconstruction, valued at buy-out); the other schemes are held as totals in version 1. CfD rows group a station’s separate per-phase contracts into one physical asset, expandable to the individual contracts. RO per-station figures are buy-out value only — the directly-sourced per-generator basis — so they understate the full cost by the recycle element that the RO scheme total includes; the bottom-up RO buy-out reconstruction (≈£59.9bn) reconciles with our recycle-inclusive scheme total (£74.6bn), the difference being recycle value plus the 2002–07 years the reconstruction does not cover.

Constraint payments (paid to switch off)

Source: Elexon BMRS, balancing settlement bid stacks · cadence: daily · history: REF published annual totals

Formula in words: for each settlement period, the accepted balancing-mechanism bids of wind units are summed: the cost is the bid volume multiplied by the bid price, and the curtailed volume is the energy the unit was instructed not to generate.

All accepted bids from wind BM units are counted, regardless of soFlag. The soFlag governs imbalance-price tagging, not whether the unit is paid.

History before the bottom-up settlement window comes from the Renewable Energy Foundation’s published annual constraint totals. Bilateral trades agreed outside the balancing mechanism are excluded, so the constraint figures are a lower bound on what is actually paid for curtailment.

Renewables Obligation (RO)

Formula in words: annual official scheme values from Ofgem’s Renewables Obligation annual reports — the number of ROCs presented multiplied by the worth of a ROC, or the headline scheme value where Ofgem states it directly. These are official annual totals pending per-generator reconstruction.

Feed-in Tariffs (FIT)

Formula in words: annual official scheme values from Ofgem’s Feed-in Tariffs annual reports. These are official annual totals pending per-generator reconstruction.

Perspectives: renewables, and all low-carbon

The dashboard's headline counts renewable generators — wind, solar, hydro and the biomass plants (Drax and the like) that hold renewable contracts and count towards the UK's renewable targets. The all-low-carbon view adds the one kind of low-carbon generation that is not renewable: nuclear. Hinkley Point C has not yet generated, so its CfD payments stand at : the all-low-carbon total () is for now identical to the renewables headline (), and the gap will open only once Hinkley begins to pay out. Indirect costs (network, balancing, carbon pricing, capacity) are tracked separately and always marked estimated. The dashboard's Include indirect costs switch controls whether they are added to the headline counter; it starts on, so the figure you first see is the combined total, and the counter's own caption says so. Switch it off and the counter falls back to the direct, measured payments alone.

Indirect subsidies (estimated)

Beyond the direct schemes above, renewable generation imposes costs that reach bill-payers and taxpayers through network charges, balancing costs, carbon pricing and the capacity levy. The dashboard shows these as ‘the indirect bill’, always marked estimated: each component’s amount is measured from an official source — recent years are verified against primary sources, while the 2009–2011 emissions-trading figures rest on the Renewable Energy Foundation’s published tabulation of OBR receipts pending primary confirmation, as noted in that component’s source notes — and the share attributable to renewables is a stated judgement. The attribution rule for each component is quoted verbatim below. From 2017 onwards the BSUoS series is measured from NESO’s settled data; the hand-maintained years end at 2016 because the settled data begins on 1 April 2016 and so covers no complete calendar year before 2017.

Capacity Market

The Capacity Market pays power stations, batteries, interconnectors and demand response to be available at times of system stress; bill-payers fund it through a levy. Any power system needs backup for the coldest, stillest evening, so the whole levy is not a cost of net zero. Since 13 October 2026 the Clock counts only the part net zero caused, the same rule it applies to network and balancing charges, which count only growth above a pre-renewables baseline.

What is counted. For each year: the Capacity Market actually paid (LCCC), less what the same system with net-zero policy removed would have paid for its own backup at that year’s capacity price. That system keeps the actual demand, nuclear, interconnectors and pumped storage; builds no wind or solar; keeps coal open where it pays; and builds gas to meet the remaining peak. Its own capacity bill is the actual price per kilowatt times the firm capacity it would have contracted. New and kept-open plant in that system are already charged at their full build and keep-open cost in the model, so they are not charged a capacity payment as well. The resulting share is applied to each month’s payment in that year.

Why the figure is a floor. The system without net-zero policy is charged the capacity price that actually cleared. Since 2021 a 550 g/kWh emissions limit has kept the 11 GW of coal that bid into earlier four-year-ahead auctions out of them (Ofgem, Annual Report on the Operation of the Capacity Market 2020/21 and 2021/22), and clearing prices rose after it. Without net zero that coal would have bid and held prices down. No published source measures how much of the price rise net zero caused, so none of it is counted.

Range. Low: kept-open coal in the system without net zero would also have needed a capacity agreement. High: the whole levy, the rule the Clock used before 13 October 2026. Both are shown below. The shares for 2016–2025 come from Richard Lyon’s model of the system without net-zero policy, which is not yet published; it will be linked here when it is. 2026 onward uses the 2025 share until the model is run for those years. The per-year shares, the amounts and their provenance are published in breakdown.json (netzero_share) and summarised on the change log. Attribution confidence stays medium.

Climate Change Levy and Carbon Price Support

The Climate Change Levy and Carbon Price Support are two distinct taxes, not the same instrument: both are captured here, and identified separately. Conservative direction: only electricity’s share of main-rate CCL declarations is counted — gas and solid-fuel CCL is excluded — plus Carbon Price Support, which falls wholly on electricity generation. The figure errs low.

Emissions trading (UK and EU ETS)

Conservative direction: only auctioned allowance revenue is counted, scaled by the power sector’s share of verified covered emissions. Power generation receives no free allocation, so it buys a larger share of auctioned allowances than its emissions share — the figure is a lower bound.

This apportionment is an assumption, not a published statistic. The revenue and emissions inputs are read from primary sources, but no publication attributes UK ETS auction revenue or auction volumes to individual sectors, so the power sector's share of that revenue is inferred from its share of verified emissions. It is the weakest step in this figure, and it is why the scheme is marked estimated rather than verified. Two gaps follow from the sources themselves: 2025 emissions by sector are not yet published — the relevant DESNZ report is expected in late 2026 — and no primary document gives a power-sector figure for 2021 at all, so that year's share cannot be traced.

Transmission charges (TNUoS)

Conservative direction: only the growth in transmission revenue above the inflation-indexed pre-renewables baseline is attributed, and the uplift is floored at zero in any year where revenue falls below the baseline.

Balancing costs (BSUoS)

Conservative direction: as for TNUoS, only the uplift above the inflation-indexed baseline is attributed, floored at zero.

Corrected 30 September 2026. This series previously came from NESO’s Daily Balancing Costs dataset, which breaks down balancing-mechanism and ancillary spend. That is not the same quantity as the balancing cost recovered from bill-payers through BSUoS: it ran 23–39% below the outturn in NESO’s own Annual Balancing Costs Report in every year from 2018-19. Because the pre-2017 history and the baseline are both on the gross BSUoS basis, the old series joined two different definitions mid-run. The series now reads NESO’s settled BSUoS charge data — the half-hourly charge up to 31 March 2023, and actual BSUoS cost thereafter, because the April 2023 fixed-tariff reform broke the identity between what users are charged and what balancing costs. Full detail, and the effect on the totals, is on the corrections page.

Paid to switch on (constraint turn-up)

When wind farms are paid to switch off behind a network bottleneck, the grid operator also pays other stations, mostly gas, to switch on and replace the lost energy. That cost is recovered through BSUoS, so it was already inside the balancing uplift. It is now taken out of the uplift and shown as its own line; the balancing line falls by the same amount and no total changes.

The figure is an estimate, built daily from Elexon settlement data for every half-hour since November 2015, using the method of Octopus Energy's Wasted Wind tracker. In each half-hour, the volume of wind bids accepted as constraint actions is priced against that half-hour's accepted offers: offers not marked as constraint actions first, then in the order they were accepted, with a part-share of the offer that crosses the volume. Short-duration offers are left out, as Octopus leaves them out. We reproduce Octopus's published figures to within 0.1% (2025: £1,466.8m against £1,467.0m). It is counted with the indirect costs, not the direct payments, because the money goes to the replacement stations, not to renewable generators, and because it is an estimate rather than a record of payments.

As a cross-check we also total what was actually paid on offers marked as constraint actions, from stations other than wind farms, in the half-hours when wind was being switched off. That figure is lower than the estimate, because much of the replacement energy is bought through offers not marked as constraint actions; it is a floor, not a contradiction. NESO's own thermal-constraint cost is higher than the estimate (2025: £1,831m), because it covers every thermal constraint, not only replacing switched-off wind. Conservative direction: the line can never exceed the balancing uplift it is taken from.

The double-counting guard

Constraint payments to wind farms are counted in the direct layer (‘paid to switch off’). Those same payments are recovered from bill-payers through BSUoS, so the BSUoS series is netted of direct constraint payments before attribution — the same pound is never counted twice across the direct and indirect layers.

Indirect costs not counted

  • Free ETS allowance allocations (a transfer forgone, not a cash flow counted here)
  • SECR reporting and corporate PPA effects on power prices
  • Smart Export Guarantee (SEG) payments
  • Offtaker of Last Resort (OLR) arrangements

Share of the electricity bill

The "Subsidy as a share of the electricity bill" chart expresses the renewable-energy subsidy as a fraction of total UK electricity consumer expenditure. The denominator is DUKES 1.3, Sales of electricity and gas by sector (annual, calendar-year).

The numerator is this site's own subsidy total, which is a deliberately conservative lower bound, so the share is a lower bound too. The 2022–23 energy-price spike raised total electricity expenditure and so lowers the subsidy share in those years even though the subsidy itself kept rising. What is not included

The totals deliberately exclude several subsidy mechanisms, so they are conservative:

  • Smart Export Guarantee payments
  • REGO certificate costs
  • Heat schemes: the Renewable Heat Incentive (RHI) and Boiler Upgrade Scheme (BUS)
  • Transport: the Renewable Transport Fuel Obligation (RTFO)
  • Energy-efficiency obligations (ECO)

The recipients map and asset panels

The map shows one marker per station per scheme (a plant paid under both CfD and RO appears once per scheme), positioned by the station’s coordinates, with marker area proportional to cumulative payment. Clicking a marker opens that asset’s panel. Definitions used in the panel:

  • Payment per subsidised MWh is cumulative CfD payment divided by cumulative CfD generation — the average realised top-up per megawatt-hour. It is not a levelised cost. It is only shown when cumulative payment and generation are both positive.
  • Quarterly chart: LCCC daily per-contract settlement summed by calendar quarter across the station’s contracts. Negative quarters are real: the market price exceeded the strike price and the generator paid back.
  • Contract status comes from the LCCC CfD Contract Portfolio Status dataset, placed verbatim.
  • Outage history comes from Elexon’s REMIT transparency feed, matched to each station’s registered Balancing Mechanism units. Only sustained, material outages are drawn: at least 24 hours with at least 20% of the unit’s normal capacity unavailable. Records begin in 2016; earlier periods are not covered, and stations without a registered BM unit (smaller, distribution-connected farms) show no outage history rather than a false “none”.
  • Regulatory notes appear only where an official document exists (Ofgem, LCCC), are close paraphrases of that document, and link to it.

Run-rate semantics

The per-second rates that drive the live counter are derived from each scheme’s recent run-rate: the trailing 365 days for daily schemes (CfD and constraints), the last 12 months for the Capacity Market, and the latest completed scheme year for RO and FIT. Values shown between official data updates are therefore estimates, and are replaced by official figures as they are published.

In today’s money, the yearly cost, the cost per household, per second and per MWh add up each line’s run-rate converted to 2024 prices from that line’s own year: the year in the middle of the twelve months it covers. A line whose latest year is 2024, such as the Renewables Obligation for April 2024 to March 2025, is already in 2024 money and is not converted again; the lines running to within weeks of today are converted with the latest price index.

The headline counts today's money: each year's cost converted to 2024 prices with the ONS CPIH index, so that a pound paid in 2002 and a pound paid in 2026 are worth the same. The equivalence figures

The dashboard's comparison lines use deliberately conservative denominators, and every count divides the quoted £230bn+ floor — not the raw total — then rounds down:

  • NHS nurses — Band 5 annual salary at the top of the band (Agenda for Change, England, 2026/27: £39,043). Using the top of the band lowers the count.
  • Social homes — gross build cost including land: £393,333 per home, from Cebr's costing for Shelter and the National Housing Federation (2024: £35.4bn for 90,000 homes). The full cost is used, not the far lower public grant per home.
  • Hinkley Point C — EDF's £35bn estimate is in 2015 prices; the Clock converts it to 2024 prices with the same ONS CPIH series it uses everywhere (about £46.5bn) before dividing. Dividing £230bn by the unadjusted £35bn would give 6 stations; the like-for-like comparison gives 4.
  • Per MWh — divides by electricity finally consumed by end users (273.8 TWh/year), not total demand including network losses (320.2 TWh), so the figure is not inflated by losses.

Data freshness and the running total

Official figures arrive late, and not all at once. The Renewables Obligation and Feed-in Tariff reports run to the end of a scheme year in March and appear about a year later; the tax and grid figures run to the end of a calendar year; the daily sources run to within days of today. The published total counts each line to its own latest official data, and nothing beyond it.

Report No. 1 counts every line to 31 December 2024, the last date to which every line is published, and the Clock runs on from there: the line under the counter shows that total, the data published since, and the estimate described below.

The counter starts from that published total. For each line it then adds the cost since that line’s latest data, at the line’s latest annual rate: the rate a year, times the time since the data end, divided by 365.25 days. In today’s money this estimate is converted at the latest price index, because the money falls after the data end. So the counter shows two parts added together: what is published, and an estimate of what has been spent since.

The estimate is replaced, not added to. When a source publishes a new period, that period enters the published total and the line’s estimate starts again from the new data end, so no day is counted twice. If the published figure differs from the estimate, the counter moves by the difference. The annual chart and the year-by-year tables show published figures only, with the latest year marked as partial. The rule is the one set out in Technical Paper No. 1, section 8, which shows the same total with the estimate added.

Each scheme on the dashboard carries a badge showing its update cadence and the latest date its data covers. Every source publishes in arrears — LCCC releases daily CfD data weekly, NESO publishes settled BSUoS data roughly three weeks behind the days it covers, and Capacity Market monthly data follows the settlement calendar — so a publication lag is normal and is not flagged. A scheme is marked stale only when its latest data is older than that scheme’s normal publication lag: 21 days for CfD, 3 days for constraints, 30 days for BSUoS, 75 days after the end of the latest data month for the Capacity Market, and 2 years for RO and FIT annual reports.

The BSUoS threshold was 7 days until 30 September 2026, when the series moved to NESO’s settled BSUoS data. That source is slower than the daily extract it replaced — about three weeks behind rather than one — because settlement runs take time to finalise. The Clock accepts roughly twelve days less timeliness for a figure that measures the right quantity; see corrections.

Restatements

Source datasets are revised by their publishers. When a source revises history, the change is logged as a restatement; figures are never silently overwritten.

That log covers the sources this site fetches automatically. It distinguishes a publisher restating its own history from a change of basis made here: the BSUoS entries dated 30 September 2026 are the latter, and are labelled as ours with the cause named. Several series are maintained by hand from annual publications, and those are revised too. On 1 September 2026 the electricity spending denominator was moved to the DUKES 2026 edition, published 30 July 2026, which revised its own history from 2010 onwards: 2024 consumer spending on electricity rose from £65.58bn to £66.19bn (+0.94%), 2023 rose £686m, and 2022 fell £222m. The figures previously shown were a correct reading of the 2025 edition, so this is a restatement by the publisher rather than an error here. DESNZ has said it plans a methodology review of this table, so further revision is likely in the 2027 edition.

Because publishers restate, a figure is only meaningful alongside the edition it came from, and this site now records that edition with each hand-maintained figure. Two related cautions apply to anyone checking the workings. Government asset URLs are not stable citations: the address that served the 2025 DUKES workbook now redirects to the 2026 file, so re-fetching it to verify an older figure silently returns different data. Superseded editions are therefore cited here through the UK Government Web Archive.
